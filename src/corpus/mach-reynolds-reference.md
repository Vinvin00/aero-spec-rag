# Mach Number, Speed of Sound, and Reynolds Number

## Speed of sound

The speed of sound in a perfect gas depends only on temperature, as the square
root of the ratio of specific heats times the specific gas constant times the
absolute temperature. Because ISA temperature falls with altitude through the
troposphere, so does the speed of sound; a vehicle holding constant true
airspeed therefore climbs into a higher Mach number.

| Quantity | Value | Unit | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| speed of sound | 340.3 | m/s | ISA sea level, 288.15 K |
| speed of sound | 295.1 | m/s | ISA tropopause, 216.65 K |
| ratio of specific heats | 1.4 | dimensionless | dry air |
| specific gas constant | 287.05 | J/(kg*K) | dry air |

## Flow regimes by Mach number

Subsonic flow below about Mach 0.8 is effectively free of shocks. The transonic
range from roughly Mach 0.8 to 1.2 contains mixed subsonic and supersonic
pockets and is where drag rise is steepest. Supersonic flow runs from about
Mach 1.2 to 5, and above Mach 5 real-gas and heating effects make the
hypersonic regime qualitatively different.

## Reynolds number

Reynolds number is density times velocity times a characteristic length divided
by dynamic viscosity, and it sets whether the boundary layer is laminar or
turbulent. For a body a metre or two long flying at several hundred metres per
second near sea level, Reynolds number is in the tens of millions, comfortably
turbulent. Wind tunnel tests on scale models rarely match that, which is why
sub-scale coefficients transfer imperfectly to full scale.

| Quantity | Value | Unit | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| dynamic viscosity | 0.00001789 | Pa*s | dry air at 288.15 K |
